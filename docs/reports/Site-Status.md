# Site Status Control

`sites.status` existed. `SiteContextInterface::isActive()` existed. **Nothing in the public path read
either**: a deactivated site served its pages, its feeds and its sitemap exactly like an active one.
The switch the PRD asked for was a column nobody consulted.

---

## What it does

`/admin/sites` now has a **Status** column: a badge, and a Deactivate / Activate button.

- **Deactivating** purges that site's page cache and records `site.status_changed` in the audit log.
- A public request to an inactive site answers **`404`** — not a maintenance page, not a message that
  the site exists but is switched off. A stranger learns nothing.
- The **feeds stop too** (`/feed`, `/atom.xml`): a reader keeps polling long after people stop visiting.
- The **admin stays reachable** at the site's own domain, so the operator can switch it back on.

`SiteService::setStatus()` validates against `SiteService::STATUSES` (`active`, `inactive`), so
`disabled` is an error rather than a third state nobody defined.

---

## The two bugs behind it

**1. The check ran before the cache, or not at all.** My first version resolved the site *before* the
page cache. That answers `404` correctly and quietly destroys the property the whole cache design
exists for: a cached page would now cost a query. The check belongs after the cache miss, and
deactivation is what makes the cached entry disappear — `SiteController::status` purges the site when
it switches it off. A cached page cannot outlive the status it was cached under.

**2. `SiteResolver::resolveById()` memoised the site.** The context was cached for the life of the
process, and the process outlives a status change: deactivate, then render, and the resolver handed
back the *active* context. Host lookups are immutable and still memoised; the mutable state is not.

## Known limitations

1. **No maintenance page.** A deactivated site is indistinguishable from a missing one. That is a
   deliberate choice for a stranger, and a poor one for an operator who would like to say "back
   soon" — a themed maintenance mode would be a separate feature.
2. **No scheduling.** Deactivation is manual; nothing switches a site off at a time.
3. **No notification.** The site's users are not told their site was switched off.
4. **Admin access continues.** Deactivation hides public output, not the admin — the site's own
   administrators can still sign in. That is intentional (otherwise nobody could undo it), and it is
   worth knowing: "inactive" means "not publishing", not "locked".
5. **Sitemap is not gated.** `sitemap.xml` still answers for an inactive site; it is generated by
   cron and served from a file.
6. **Cron keeps rebuilding** feeds and sitemaps for inactive sites; they are just not served.
